# Architecture dependence of task-admission coalescing conclusions

**Decision.** The transferable contribution is a conditional one: batching new-task admission changes both request waiting and the workload presented to a continuing allocator, so admission frequency alone cannot predict total computation or service. The measured HIPC, CBAA, and RP2040 signs are valuable counterexamples and operating points, but their exact ordering is tied to this controller, its call triggers, state retention, finite tail, and timing model. A processor-speed crossover has **not** been identified. The corrected results are sufficient for a RoboticCC paper that states those limits; no broader new campaign is needed.

This audit uses the corrected 50-trace, 50-task AGX and exact zero-compute records; the four-trace RP2040 medium-load HIPC pair; the producing AGX source `598485103ee270a27d8b684c0082e46e2ad53802`; and an isolated, zero-time no-recovery diagnostic. It does not replace any production outcome. [Anchor calculations](anchor_metric_comparisons.csv), [their script and definitions](ANCHOR_DATA_NOTES.md), [source-by-source architecture notes](architecture_notes.md), and the [focused literature check](literature_notes.md) are supporting material. The existing [RoboticCC handoff](../roboticcc_handoff/ROBOTICCC_PAPER_HANDOFF.md) supplies completion accounting, paired intervals, and the broader policy landscape. Effects below are policy minus Eager on the same completed traces; AGX anchors have 50 pairs unless stated otherwise. Work is summed **measured allocator transaction duration** on a processor-work accounting boundary, not OS CPU time, mission delay, or energy.

## What should transfer without this simulator

For exogenous release times `r_i`, let admission occur at `a_i >= r_i`. The environmental wait is `q_i = a_i - r_i`. A count threshold `B` normally creates fewer admission epochs than admitting each release, but a task waits for later releases; a timeout `W` bounds that gate wait if its deadline is honored. Under a stationary Poisson stream and full count batches, the familiar illustrative mean is approximately `(B-1)/(2λ)` for arrival rate `λ`. That estimate excludes an initial task set, finite tails, altered service trajectories, and any terminal rule. It predicts why count batching becomes costly at low arrival load, not the exact measured latency.

Let `E_P` denote policy `P`'s admission epochs, `k_e` its direct allocator calls caused by epoch `e`, and `A_P` calls caused by other events. Then `C_P = Σ_e k_e + A_P`, while total work is exactly `W_P = Σ_{j=1}^{C_P} c_j`, approximately `C_P × mean(c_P)`. A batch can reduce `E_P` and still raise `A_P`, `mean(c_P)`, or both. Its work break-even depends on fixed invocation overhead, cost growth with the admitted/active set, retained state, and the number of downstream consensus and repair calls. Merely calling an allocator “heavier” is insufficient: high fixed call overhead favors batching, while sharply superlinear batch processing can make it worse. A slower processor increases the time represented by work only to the extent that computing sits on a robot's service-critical path. Parallel robots, movement, buffered events, and route changes can weaken or reverse a mission-time inference.

These statements do not assume this simulator's four robots, algorithms, or message bus. The existence of a waiting-versus-batching tension is independently consistent with [time-window versus count warehouse batching](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpe.2009.01.013) and [online window choices across batching/routing methods](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207543.2023.2263884). Those systems are analogies, not demonstrations of MRTA processor-work signs. [CBBA partial replanning](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.04836) and [incremental dynamic MRTA solving](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.11737) independently show that update scope and retained state can materially alter runtime.

## Architecture choices that determine the empirical signs

The [detailed source audit](architecture_notes.md) gives line-level references and an endpoint-impact table. The mechanisms most consequential to the proposed claims are these:

| Corrected implementation choice | How changing it could change calls, work, latency, mission time, or policy ordering |
|---|---|
| Admission is a reliable, delayed environmental announcement to all four robots; each robot requests a safe-boundary call while retaining motion and goal. | Direct epoch-listed calls scale with robot count and admission frequency. Lossy admission, central allocation, or immediate preemption would change all five endpoints. One epoch is not one call. |
| Calls continue on allocator messages, peer-state change, completion, invalid goal, quarantine expiry, and stalled no-goal recovery. Multiple calls can follow one admission, while a call with no admission context has `epoch_id=None`. | Autonomous calls can overwhelm fewer direct admission calls; they alter work and can change routes, service, mission duration, and ranking. Epoch-listed versus other calls is an accounting split, **not** a causal trigger partition. |
| One queued context is consumed per call, but queued admission hooks may be drained together. No admission resets allocator ownership, path, or executing goal; an active-set cache is invalidated. | Changing hook coalescence or resetting state changes call count and per-call cost. It could reverse HIPC/CBAA work rankings and responsiveness. |
| CBAA retains a won single goal and bid; HIPC recomputes a local team plan over eligible tasks and reconciles a suffix. Completion repair is allocator-specific. | The same larger admitted set has different marginal cost and different autonomous message consequences. “Compute-heavy benefits more” is not an allocator-class theorem. |
| A no-goal recovery deadline is local (120 s), and a Count sub-`B` terminal residual waits until no future release and all earlier admitted tasks are physically complete. | Recovery can add calls or prevent stalling in other regimes; the final residual can enlarge waiting, tail latency, and mission time. These rules can alter ordering even with identical releases. |
| Each robot has an independent virtual processor. Its current transaction blocks that robot; peer movement, releases, and messages continue. Inputs arriving during compute are buffered. | Summed processor work is not a globally blocking mission delay. A serialized server or interruptible movement would change timing feedback, call paths, latency, and possibly work. |
| Timed work includes queued allocator hooks/input processing, optional recovery, and goal selection. It excludes transport/serialization, routing, and other host work. Peer communication can be delayed/lost; admission itself is protected. | Broader compute boundaries or different networks could change measured work and ranking. AGX elapsed transaction duration, zero injected duration, and RP2040 device duration are distinct treatments. |

In particular, HIPC's per-call team-plan recomputation and CBAA's retained-head behavior make absolute allocator weights and policy responses specific to these adapted implementations. Reliable admission isolates a clean policy intervention, but a system in which task announcements can be lost is a different experiment. The finite stopping rules also matter: 60 corrected AGX missions did not complete under the configured event/stagnation horizons, so all continuous comparisons below use complete pairs and must not be interpreted as unconditional policy rankings. The [failure dossier](../roboticcc_handoff/diagnostics/report.md) distinguishes stopping from proven deadlock.

**HIPC/high Bounded** is the strongest complete AGX work example, selected after examining results: work falls on 38/50 traces, by a mean 0.325 processor-s and median paired 23.61%; its all-50-task mean latency change is +1.405 s with a pointwise 95% interval spanning −0.215 to +3.147 s. Online-only latency rises by 1.566 s on average. Admission-to-completion for online tasks actually falls from 30.854 to 29.735 s; added admission wait of 2.686 s exceeds that downstream improvement. The work saving is small beside a roughly 140 s mission and does not establish service equivalence or faster completion. Calls directly listed on admission epochs fall 172 → 48.48; other calls also fall 837.90 → 787.02. The latter are not all independent of admissions.

**CBAA/high Count B4** supplies a complete counterexample to treating admissions as compute. Directly epoch-listed calls fall 172 → 48, while calls *not listed* on an epoch rise 289.08 → 447.36. Total calls rise 461.08 → 495.36 and mean work/call rises 0.304 → 0.348 ms; whole-mission work rises by only 0.0321 s (42/50 traces). The mission also lengthens 117.78 → 142.18 s. Work per unit simulated mission time changes only +1.50% and is higher on 25/50 traces; thus extra total work partly reflects a longer exposure period, not a clear rise in compute intensity. Online release-to-completion rises by 5.187 s and still rises by 3.197 s on the identical first 40 online tasks. The two final residual tasks amplify but do not create the latency penalty. No available aggregate identifies which particular consensus/completion branch caused each extra call, and a 32 ms work increase cannot by itself explain 24 s of mission extension.

**Low-load Count B4** demonstrates a different sign combination: CBAA's calls and work fall 24.67% and 29.39%, while online latency rises 150.85% because admission wait averages 19.339 s. Its first-40-task latency remains worse. All four allocators' low-load Count B4 all-50 mean latency is worse on every one of their 50 paired traces, though these are the same underlying environmental traces across allocators. A timeout mitigates the queue component: HIPC/low Bounded B4/W10 has mean online wait 7.584 s and latency 21.359 s, versus Count B4's 19.412 s and 34.257 s; CBAA/low shows 7.570 and 20.900 s versus 19.339 and 33.972 s. Bounded remains slower than Eager in those examples. The exact values depend on arrival traces, route changes, and the terminal rule, while the timeout's gate-wait bound is general under a functioning clock.

**First-goal and normalization caveats.** Admission-to-first-assignment is available for all 42 online tasks in all selected AGX complete pairs: HIPC/high is 0.588 → 0.544 s and CBAA/high 18.904 → 18.539 s. First-current-goal events are absent for some completed tasks. A paired-observed-task comparison covers on average 39.66/42 HIPC and 38.54/42 CBAA online indices; all-42 complete-goal pairs are only 6/50 and 2/50, respectively. Their sign is selection-sensitive, so no population-level admission-to-first-goal improvement is claimed. Whole-mission work per admitted task and per released online task appear in the CSV, but dividing by the fixed 50 or 42 tasks merely rescales whole-mission work; it is not a separately identified online-work effect. Mission-time normalization uses each mission's actual duration and answers a different question from total work.

An “admission-trigger-only” run was **not** created: suppressing consensus/outbid/completion transactions would alter the allocator's convergence and task correctness, rather than change a nonessential scheduling detail. ## Literature boundary and experiment decision

The closest external work changes **different knobs**: [event-driven CBBA](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.06481v1) suppresses communication after tasks are known; [CBBA partial replanning](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.04836) changes repair scope; [dynamic SMT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.11737) retains solver learning; [warehouse batching](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207543.2023.2263884) delays orders before dispatch. They independently support waiting, update-scope, and method-dependent cost, but none proves the present CBAA call reversal or an RP2040 speed threshold. The [literature notes](literature_notes.md) give source-specific answers and limits. Do not claim novelty from a different simulator alone, or equate fewer broadcasts with less CPU work.

**Recommendation: No additional experiment needed for RoboticCC**, provided the paper centers the conditional admission/work/responsiveness insight and scopes algorithm and hardware numbers to the corrected setup. The existing exact-zero arm, 50-trace trigger decomposition, first-40 residual analysis, and no-recovery check resolve the most immediate artifact hypotheses without inventing another allocator. If a stronger claim that the CBAA reversal survives different consensus triggers is essential, the smallest informative future test would use two or three frozen high-load traces, Eager/Count B4, and a separately validated alternative trigger scheduler that preserves the allocator's required convergence and completion behavior. The reversal would need to persist in total calls and work after matched completion; a sign change would force the claim to remain explicitly trigger-architecture-specific. That is a different, optional claim, not a prerequisite for the bounded conclusion above.

## Claims safe to present as general MRTA insights

- Temporal admission can lower invocation opportunities while adding release-to-admission wait; a timeout bounds that gate wait when honored.
- Total allocator work depends on **both** the number of calls and the cost of each call, including downstream autonomous activity. Admission count alone is an inadequate compute surrogate.
- Arrival load, update scope/state retention, and processor critical-path exposure are plausible moderators; no sign of a work, service, or mission-time change follows from batching alone.

## Only claims that must be scoped to this implementation

- High-load HIPC B4/W10's measured 0.325 s mean work saving and its uncertain all-task latency change; high-load CBAA Count B4's extra 34.28 calls and 0.0321 s total work despite 31 fewer epochs; all exact policy rankings and thresholds.
- The high-load CBAA non-epoch-listed call increase, its near-flat work per mission second, and any causal account of internal consensus/completion branches. The archived data do not identify work by trigger.
- The 42-online-task terminal-residual effect, retained CBAA/ HIPC state and cache/repair behavior, protected admission and lossy peer messages, four independent virtual processors, and this allocator timer boundary.
- The RP2040 medium-HIPC result as a four-trace same-board/same-lineage observation. A processor-speed crossover, hardware-general policy optimum, energy benefit, and cross-allocator “heavier always benefits more” claim remain unestablished.

## Recommended wording for the RoboticCC paper

> Temporal coalescing of newly released tasks creates a conditional work–responsiveness tradeoff in decentralized online allocation: fewer admission epochs can reduce allocator invocations, but the resulting task wait and changes in autonomous calls and per-call cost determine total work and service latency.

> In our corrected four-robot implementation, high-load HIPC with bounded B4/W10 admission reduced summed allocator transaction work relative to Eager, whereas high-load CBAA with Count B4 increased total calls and work despite far fewer admissions. Sparse-arrival Count B4 imposed substantial task waiting; a 10 s timeout reduced that waiting. These measured signs and operating points are specific to the tested allocators, trigger rules, retained state, and workload.

> On four matched medium-load HIPC RP2040 traces, Count B4 improved work and task latency in three pairs. Because the AGX and RP2040 executions also differ in communication and runtime details, this comparison does not isolate processor speed or establish a portable hardware break-even point.
